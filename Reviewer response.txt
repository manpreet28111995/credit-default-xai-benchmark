RESPONSE TO REVIEWERS
Credit default prediction beyond AUROC

AUTHOR NOTE: This draft answers comments on the previous version using the
current repository. No manuscript was supplied for checking revisions or
page/line locations. Available evidence and proposed manuscript changes are
identified separately. No new training or explainer execution was performed
when preparing this response. Before submission, reconcile the text with the
actual manuscript, add exact revision locations, and remove this author note.
Requests requiring unavailable experiments are explicitly partially addressed.

Dear Editor and Reviewers,

Thank you for identifying the need for a clearer contribution, broader evidence,
and explicit robustness, explainability, and fairness assessment. We agree that
the individual techniques are established. The contribution we can support is
an empirical comparison of their trade-offs under documented protocols, rather
than a new algorithm or a validated trustworthiness measure.

The current result package contains six classifier families evaluated in 32 runs:
ten random splits of UCI Default of Credit Card Clients, ten random splits of
South German Credit, and six Prosper temporal cutoffs with two seeds per cutoff.
This extends the UCI-only evidence described in the review. It does not establish
universal generalization. Below, we distinguish available evidence from limits
that remain when further training and explanation runs are unavailable.


REVIEWER 1

General comment: The contribution is incremental and requires substantial
rewriting to improve originality, clarity, interpretation, and comparison with
existing approaches.

Response:
We accept the criticism of the contribution's presentation. The defensible
contribution is a comparison of how model assessments differ across
discrimination, probability quality, thresholds, synthetic perturbation
sensitivity, explanation diagnostics, and group disparities. We do not claim
novelty for the individual methods or for multi-dimensional evaluation itself.

For example, CatBoost and LightGBM both have mean UCI AUROC 0.7879, but their mean
Brier scores are 0.1461 and 0.1324. On Prosper, CatBoost leads mean AUROC at 0.6822,
whereas LightGBM has the lowest mean Brier score, 0.0618. A discrimination-based
choice therefore need not be the best choice for probability quality. These
comparisons give readers an interpretation beyond the model-ranking table.

Proposed manuscript wording:
"This study provides an empirical comparison of six classifier families across
three credit datasets. It examines how discrimination-based comparisons differ
from assessments based on threshold-dependent decisions, probability quality,
synthetic perturbations, explanation diagnostics, and group disparities. The
findings concern the evaluated portfolios and protocols; they do not establish
a new benchmarking standard or a validated measure of trustworthiness."

Evidence: Dataset-level runs/summary/all_run_metric_summary.csv files and
per-run run_manifest.json files under results/outputs/.


Comment 1: Compare robustness and integrate accuracy, explainability, and
robustness into a single reliability score, considering
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mlwa.2025.100821 and related work.

Response:
Available held-out perturbation analyses cover all six classifier families.
They evaluate numeric missingness replacement, correlated block replacement,
noise, and additive covariate shifts at levels 0.05, 0.10, and 0.20. Outputs
include classification metrics, Brier error, probability changes, and decision
flips. These assess specified synthetic sensitivities, not actual economic
shocks, adversarial security, or every form of distribution shift.

The existing champion-level composite has the form

    R = (P * S * E * F)^(1/4).

P summarizes discrimination and nonnegative Brier skill; S summarizes AUROC
retention under the specified perturbations; E combines explanation diagnostics;
and F is the inverse of the maximum reported group gap at the nearest evaluated
threshold. P is the square root of AUROC times nonnegative Brier skill. E averages
rescaled LIME repeatability, SHAP attribution concentration, absolute deletion
probability change, and nonnegative LIME local R-squared. Scaling and clipping
rules are defined in pipeline.py and evaluation/revision_analysis.py.

Equal weighting and these transformations are modelling choices, not validated
measures of trust or operational risk. A zero score means that a component is
zero under this construction, not that the model is useless. Missing fairness,
as on Prosper, leaves the composite unavailable rather than imputing fairness.
Components should accompany this supplementary descriptive score.

SAFE AI metrics [1] motivate integrated evaluation, but our construction does
not reproduce their perturbation-based metrics or validate that method. It is
not evidence of compliance or deployment readiness.

This request is partially addressed. XAI diagnostics exist only for champions;
therefore the composite does not compare all six families on a common explanation
basis. No run identifies a feasible reliability-selected model. For Prosper,
feasibility cannot be established because group attributes are missing, which
is not a measured fairness violation. Selection-side robustness also uses an
in-sample training subset, not independent validation observations.

Proposed manuscript treatment: Retain classifier-level perturbation results in
the main analysis. Keep the composite and components supplementary, with its
coverage, construction choices, and lack of validation explicit.

Evidence: Per-run robustness_perturbations.csv, reliability_scores.csv,
reliability_selection.csv, and revision_analysis_manifest.json;
pipeline.py, stage_revision_analyses; evaluation/revision_analysis.py,
reliability_score.


Comment 2: Compare explainability tools formally using parsimony and robustness,
considering https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-025-11141-w.

Response:
Current outputs quantify attribution concentration and the number of features
accounting for 80% of absolute attribution mass. They also report SHAP-LIME
magnitude-rank agreement on shared selected features, local LIME R-squared, and
rank/top-ten overlap across three repeated LIME seeds per evaluated case.

Mean local LIME R-squared is 0.2848 on UCI, 0.4088 on South German, and 0.2425 on
Prosper. Mean magnitude-rank correlations are 0.6044, 0.5965, and 0.3092. These are
descriptive averages over evaluated cases and runs, not independent-sample
comparisons of explainer populations. They support caution about local surrogate
fidelity, not a generally superior explanation tool.

Calzarossa, Giudici, and Zieni [2] motivate concentration as a parsimony criterion
and examine explanation variability across bootstrap-trained models. Our LIME
seed repetitions instead measure stochastic repeatability for a fixed model and
input. They do not reproduce that refitting-based robustness analysis.

Two limits remain. SHAP indices are randomly drawn and sorted, then the first
200 cases are used for agreement and the first 100 for repeated-seed diagnostics.
These subsets concentrate near the start of test-row order rather than being
uniform draws from the whole partition. This does not imply chronological
concentration in the unsorted Prosper export. LIME also retains 15 features,
so concentration comparisons depend partly on truncation. XAI covers champions,
not all classifier families.

Saved deletion/insertion metrics are absolute probability changes relative to
a training median/mode baseline. In particular, "comprehensiveness" is
abs(p_full - p_deleted), which loses direction. Without directional analysis
and random-feature controls, a large absolute change is not proof of faithfulness.
SHAP additivity checks numerical reconstruction, not causal validity or human
usefulness. Correlation of attribution magnitudes does not measure agreement
about effect direction.

This request is partially addressed through formal descriptive criteria.
Additional sampling, input-stability, and refitting checks were not performed.
The proposed conclusion concerns limitations of the measured explanations,
not their regulatory adequacy or inadequacy.

Evidence: Per-run explanation_quality_metrics.csv,
explanation_fidelity_metrics.csv, shap_lime_agreement.csv, and shap_manifest.json;
pipeline.py, stage_shap and stage_lime; evaluation/revision_analysis.py,
explanation_metrics and explanation_fidelity.


Comment 3: Compare with standard approaches through appropriate ablations.

Response:
The benchmark includes an unweighted, unresampled None arm; inverse-frequency
ClassWeight; and SMOTE, BorderlineSMOTE, SVMSMOTE, and ADASYN variants using the
project's nominal-aware sampler. It interpolates continuous features and assigns
categories by neighbour voting; it is not the library SMOTENC implementation.

Current controlled ablations compare strategies within each run's selected
champion family under fixed 0.5 thresholds, OOF-selected F1 thresholds, and
isotonic calibration fitted to training OOF predictions. UCI also compares raw
and engineered features. Champion-family tuned parameters are reused across
strategy arms rather than separately optimized for each arm. TabNet inherits
the probe strategy. These choices bound the comparisons.

Across paired strategy/run comparisons, OOF threshold selection increases mean
held-out F1 over threshold 0.5 by 0.0267 on UCI, 0.0376 on South German, and 0.1434
on Prosper, across 60, 60, and 72 comparisons. AUROC and Brier stay unchanged
because threshold selection does not change probabilities. This improves a
decision operating point rather than discrimination or probability quality.

UCI's OOF-selected champions use None in eight runs and ClassWeight in two.
This supports "synthetic oversampling provided no consistent advantage under
the evaluated UCI protocol," rather than "synthetic resampling is unnecessary"
for all credit data, objectives, or search budgets.

Corrected Prosper manifests specify skip_ablation_grid=true. Legacy
model-by-strategy grid files may remain, but their presence does not establish
regeneration under corrected selection. The evidence cited here uses current
controlled ablations, not those legacy grids.

Proposed manuscript treatment: State each changed factor, fixed parameters,
selection rule, and interpretation. Scope conclusions to the evaluated datasets,
families, and tuning budget.

Evidence: Per-run controlled_ablations.csv, best_params.yaml,
smote_ablation_summary.json, and run_manifest.json;
dataset-level runs/summary/all_run_best_models.csv.


Comment 4: Consider fairness and compare with accuracy-fairness trade-off
literature.

Response:
Available analyses measure TPR, FPR, and selection-rate gaps for recorded UCI and
South German groups. Group thresholds are fitted on training OOF predictions
and frozen before held-out evaluation. This relates to equality-of-opportunity
postprocessing [3], but meeting an OOF constraint does not guarantee a test-set
constraint.

For UCI education groups, mean held-out TPR gap decreases from 0.4302 to 0.1560
across 60 classifier/run comparisons. Only 19 meet a held-out gap of at most 0.10;
mean accuracy falls 19.32 percentage points. For sex groups, the smaller reference
gap changes from 0.0323 to 0.0233, alongside an 18.23-percentage-point mean accuracy
loss. The consequences depend on the group and operating point, rather than
establishing a universally beneficial intervention.

Utility assumes FP:FN costs of 1:5. The reference threshold maximizes F1, whereas
the group policy optimizes cost. Utility changes therefore combine objective
changes with group-specific thresholds. A matched unconstrained cost-selected
comparator has not been evaluated; gains cannot be attributed solely to fairness.

Hardt, Price, and Srebro [3] supply the postprocessing context and discuss limits
of group-statistical fairness criteria. Pleiss et al. [4] examine tensions between
calibration and error-rate constraints under their stated assumptions. Our results
illustrate the need to inspect several objectives; they do not test a universal
impossibility theorem. Group thresholds change decisions, not the calibration
of the underlying probabilities.

Some South German group gaps are undefined when a group has no positive cases.
They are missing estimates, not zero disparities. Prosper lacks group attributes
for this analysis. We cannot claim fairness coverage across all three datasets,
individual fairness, or regulatory compliance.

This request is addressed descriptively through group disparities and accuracy,
with incomplete group coverage and the unmatched utility comparator disclosed.

Evidence: Per-run oof_fitted_fairness_postprocessing.csv,
fairness_postprocessing_bootstrap_intervals.csv, fairness_utility_curve.csv,
and revision_analysis_manifest.json.


Comment 5: Rewrite the paper in a human-friendly way, explaining results and
providing intuition and mathematical explanations.

Response:
We agree that readers need interpretations alongside metric tables. The proposed
presentation organizes results around which models rank defaults well, whether
their probabilities are useful, whether thresholds account for apparent imbalance
gains, and what explanation and fairness diagnostics support.

The mathematical distinctions can be stated directly. AUROC measures ranking.
Brier error measures mean squared probability error:

    Brier = (1/n) * sum_i (p_i - y_i)^2.

The diagnostic Brier skill is

    Brier skill = 1 - Brier / [pi * (1 - pi)],

where pi is held-out prevalence for the descriptive constant-predictor reference.
This is a test-set reporting reference, not a deployable baseline with knowledge
of future prevalence. F1 depends on threshold through precision and recall,
so it can change while AUROC and Brier stay fixed. Result paragraphs should
connect numerical comparisons to these distinctions and explain their limits.

The proposed rewrite names the measured form of repeatability, labels absolute
deletion/insertion changes accurately, and separates component diagnostics from
the heuristic composite. Repetitive tables and exploratory composite details
belong in supplementary material.

Completion status: The updated README documents this interpretation and its
limits. No manuscript was supplied, so this response cannot claim a completed
substantial manuscript rewrite or provide verified page/line locations.


REVIEWER 2

Summary of the previous version: Six classifiers were benchmarked on UCI across
prediction, imbalance handling, calibration, and explainability, with conclusions
about boosting, thresholds versus SMOTE, and regulatory justification.

Response:
Thank you for identifying the scope of the earlier experiment. Current results
include the broader three-dataset evaluation described above. We distinguish
UCI-specific findings from outcomes that vary across datasets and measured
explanation limitations from regulatory suitability, which these experiments
do not establish.


Comment: The main concern is lack of novelty. Techniques are established and
empirical findings are intuitive or unsurprising.

Response:
We agree that established components should not be presented as new algorithms.
The proposed contribution is an empirical benchmark of documented trade-offs.
It is not simply that boosting can outperform logistic regression. Assessments
change with the objective and dataset, while explanation and fairness diagnostics
reveal limits that discrimination alone does not capture.

On UCI, CatBoost and LightGBM share mean AUROC 0.7879 while differing in Brier
error. On South German, Random Forest leads descriptively at mean AUROC 0.7902,
but its difference from logistic regression is not significant after within-run
Holm adjustment in any of ten runs. On Prosper, CatBoost leads mean AUROC, while
only LightGBM has positive held-out Brier skill in all twelve runs. These support
objective-dependent assessment, not a universally superior classifier.

Existing credit-scoring work compares models with SHAP [5], and loan-approval
work combines SHAP, LIME, and fairness diagnostics [6]. Recent multi-metric
credit-model work also considers calibration and stress sensitivity [7]. We do
not claim an unprecedented combination. The defensible contribution lies in
the specific comparisons, threshold/strategy ablations, temporal evaluation,
and documented limitations.

Proposed manuscript treatment: State this empirical contribution in the
introduction and conclusion. Remove unsupported "first," "novel framework,"
and universal-superiority claims rather than answer novelty criticism with
stronger promotional language.


Comment: Findings come from one dataset; generalization is a major concern.

Response:
Current evidence includes UCI, South German, and Prosper. The first two use ten
repeated stratified random splits each. Prosper uses six temporal cutoffs and
two seeds per cutoff, with a twelve-month default horizon and six-month test
windows. All twelve active Prosper revision-analysis manifests identify
ObservableTimeSeriesSplit, with month-disjoint validation and training-label
availability checks.

This broadens the evidence without establishing generalization to all borrowers
or institutions. Models are fitted separately to each dataset, not transferred
as fixed fitted models across platforms. Seeds reuse observations, the two
Prosper seeds share each test window, and expanding training sets overlap across
windows. Across-run dispersion and ranks are descriptive. Bootstrap intervals
condition on fitted models and selected thresholds.

Selection is training-only CV/OOF, not fully nested CV. Hyperparameters are
selected on folds subsequently used for OOF prediction, so OOF metrics are
selection diagnostics. Historical UCI/South German manifest labels do not alter
this interpretation. The first Prosper inner training fold has 108 loans,
including seven defaults; sensitivity to a later start remains untested.
Lending Club results are absent and are not a fourth evaluated dataset.

Proposed manuscript wording:
"The three datasets broaden the evidence beyond one portfolio, but their
populations, features, and outcome definitions differ. Repeated splits and
temporal windows provide descriptive sensitivity analyses. They do not establish
independent replication or universal generalization."


Comment: "This paper establishes a new standard for credit-scoring benchmarking"
is clearly overstated.

Response:
We agree. The results do not justify this statement. The proposed replacement is:

"This study provides a documented empirical benchmark and identifies trade-offs
that are obscured by discrimination-only comparisons."

The same scope should govern the abstract, introduction, discussion, and
conclusion. The study does not establish a new standard, validate a general
reliability measure, or certify deployment readiness.
